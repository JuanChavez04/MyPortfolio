Lab 8 – Identify the attack vectors on USB drive

Course 5 - Assets, Threats, and Vulnerabilities

Overview

In this activity, I analyzed the security risks associated with finding an unknown USB drive in a parking lot. USB baiting is a common attack technique where a threat actor leaves a malicious USB device in a public area, hoping someone will plug it into a work computer. I reviewed the contents of the USB, evaluated the risks from both an attacker and target perspective, and identified controls that could mitigate similar incidents.

Scenario

A USB stick with the hospital’s logo was found in the parking lot. Because unknown USB devices can contain malware, the security team used virtualization software to safely examine the drive. Virtual machines isolate the USB from the rest of the network, allowing analysts to inspect files without risking infection of hospital systems.

Contents

The USB drive contained a mix of personal and work-related files, including family photos, HR documents, a new‑hire letter, and employee schedules. These files may contain personally identifiable information (PII) and sensitive business data. Storing personal and work files together increases the risk of exposure if the device is lost, stolen, or accessed by unauthorized individuals.

Attacker Mindset

An attacker could use the HR documents to impersonate employees or gain access to internal hospital systems. Personal photos could be leveraged for social‑engineering attacks targeting Jorge, his coworkers, or even his relatives. The combination of personal and professional data provides multiple opportunities for phishing, identity theft, or targeted attacks against the hospital.

Risk Analysis

A malicious USB drive could contain malware designed to infect hospital computers as soon as it is plugged in. This could lead to ransomware, unauthorized access to patient records, or disruption of hospital operations. Sensitive information stored on the device could be used for identity theft or to craft convincing phishing attacks. Technical controls such as disabling autorun, enforcing endpoint protection, and scanning removable media can reduce these risks. Operational controls include employee training and policies that separate personal and work devices. Managerial controls such as encryption and access monitoring further reduce exposure.

Worksheet Evidence

Reflection

This activity reinforced the importance of treating unknown USB devices as potential threats. Even when a device appears to belong to the organization, it may contain malware or sensitive information that should not be exposed. By using virtualization, enforcing strict removable‑media policies, and educating employees, organizations can significantly reduce the risk of USB‑based attacks.
